## Is the bleeding life-threatening?  `bleeding/start`

Voice: bleeding, bleeding wont stop, heavy bleeding, severe bleeding, bad bleeding, lots of blood, blood everywhere, spurting blood, bleeding badly, help bleeding, stop the bleeding, amputation

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-5**

> The priority in caring for a casualty, should be to identify and stop life-threatening bleeding and prevent them from bleeding to death. Bleeding is considered life-threatening when --
> - There is pulsing or steady bleeding from the wound.
> - The overlying clothes are soaked with blood.
> - Blood is pooling on the ground.
> - There is a traumatic amputation of an extremity.
> - Bandages or makeshift bandages used to cover the wound are ineffective and are steadily becoming soaked with blood.
> - There was prior bleeding, and the patient is now in shock (unconscious, confused, and pale).

## Apply a tourniquet  `bleeding/tq`

Voice: tourniquet, how to use a tourniquet, apply a tourniquet, put on a tourniquet

Picture: Figure 4-5. Windlass and Ratchet Tourniquets (ATP 4-02.11, figure 4-5, 188x122)

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-37**

> In combat scenarios, especially while under enemy fire, a TQ stands as the primary means to control massive extremity bleeding, enabling individuals, battle buddies, or combat medics to swiftly manage lifethreatening hemorrhages until the casualty can be moved away from the firefight. Always prioritize treating life-threatening hemorrhage while seeking cover. The application of a TQ to a massive bleed is timesensitive, with the TQ aiming to halt bleeding within one minute and be fully secured within three minutes, casualties with uncontrolled massive bleeding can succumb within these initial minutes. Expecting pain during application is crucial as it signifies proper TQ application.

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-38**

> The casualty may need to apply a one-handed TQ (see figure 4-5) to an upper extremity when applying self-aid. One-handed TQs are used to apply self-aid for bleeding from an injury to the upper arm or forearm. (One-handed windlass and one-handed ratchet TQs are the TQs of choice; they are effective and can be applied quickly).

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-43**

> Two-Handed (Windlass) TQ Application CUF:
> - Remove TQ from JFAK and/or carrying pouch.
> - Insert the wounded extremity through the loop of the self-adhering band or route the band around the limb, passing the tip through the slit of the routing buckle.
> - Position the TQ above the bleeding site, high on the extremity over the clothing/uniform.
> - Ensure that all the slack in the self-adhering band is pulled through the routing buckle before the band is fastened back on itself and the windlass is twisted.
> - Twist the rod until bleeding has stopped (complete steps 1-5 in under 1 min).
> - Lock the windlass rod in place with the windlass clip.
> - Route the self-adhering band around the rod and between the clips.
> - Secure the windlass safety strap.
> - If no other major bleeding is present, move to cover. Note. Do not document TQ application time until the TFC phase.

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-39**

> A hasty TQ is implemented as "high and tight" on the wounded extremity or when the bleeding source is ambiguous during the CUF phase. However, during TFC, all TQs should be deliberate, placed two to three inches above the wound directly on the skin, not over clothing. Once the casualty is in a safer position, the bleeding source should be reassessed, and the effectiveness of the TQs verified. If bleeding remains uncontrolled, a second TQ may be necessary, particularly for severe bleeding wounds of the thigh.

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-40**

> It is crucial to recognize that TQs should be used cautiously, with the optimal safe duration being less than two hours. Beyond this timeframe, tissues may start to die, potentially leading to limb loss after six hours. Regular reassessment of TQ effectiveness is necessary, including checking for stopped bleeding, absent distal pulse, and ensuring no pulse below the TQ on the limb. Common errors when applying TQs include not using one, when necessary, not tightening it adequately, and using it for minimal bleeding. Additionally, TQs should never be removed except by medical personnel at a higher role of care. If the source of massive bleeding is evident, such as from an amputation or major wound, immediate bleeding control measures like applying a TQ or wound packing should be initiated, especially in areas where a TQ cannot be used, such as the groin, armpit, or neck. The following steps address tourniquet application in CUF for all Service Members, CLS, and medical personnel.

## No tourniquet  `bleeding/pack`

Voice: no tourniquet, pack a wound, wound packing, how to pack a wound, direct pressure

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-80**

> If a TQ is not available, pack the wound and hold direct pressure over the source of the heaviest bleed, FIRST. If this fails to control the bleeding in an arm or leg, only then consider an alternate improvised TQ.

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-66**

> After packing the wound with a hemostatic dressing or gauze, hold direct pressure for three minutes. Direct pressure can and should be used as a temporary measure until a TQ or dressing is in place. It is difficult to use direct pressure alone to control significant bleeding, or while moving the casualty. While packing a wound, maintain constant, direct pressure, at the source of the bleeding to be effective.

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-68**

> Wound packing:
> - Identify and expose the wound.
> - Locate the source of the most active bleeding and apply direct pressure, while preparing the hemostatic dressing.
> - Remove the hemostatic dressing from its sterile package and pack it into the wound tightly, directly over the site of the most active bleeding.
> - Fill and pack the whole wound cavity tightly while keeping firm pressure on the wound.
> - Ensure the hemostatic dressing extends one to two inches above the skin.
> - Hold continuous direct pressure for a minimum of three minutes.
> - Reassess the wound to ensure that the bleeding has stopped.
> - Apply a pressure bandage over the hemostatic dressing to secure it in place.
> - Reassess the wound to ensure that the bleeding has stopped. If blood is seeping through or around the hemostatic dressing, the bleeding is not controlled, and the following steps should be applied:
> - If extra hemostatic dressings are available, remove the initial, ineffective hemostatic dressing and replace them with new hemostatic dressings.
> - If extra hemostatic dressings are unavailable, leave initial, ineffective hemostatic dressing in place and add extra gauze over the wound tract.
> - If roller or compressed gauze was used initially, due to unavailability of hemostatic dressings, leave initial, ineffective roller or compressed gauze in place and add extra gauze over the wound tract.
> - Once bleeding has been controlled, apply a pressure bandage over the wound packing material to secure it in place. Note. More than one hemostatic dressing may be required to stop the blood flow.

## Neck bleeding  `bleeding/neck`

Voice: neck bleeding, bleeding from the neck, cut throat

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-75**

> A neck wound should be packed with a hemostatic dressing using your fingers to ensure that the gauze is packed in all parts of the wound. The wound should be packed sufficiently to ensure that the gauze extends one to two inches above the skin surface. Direct pressure must be maintained continuously for three minutes and maintained throughout the application of the dressing. A pressure dressing is applied on top of the gauze.

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-76**

> Neck junctional hemorrhage control:
> - Consider body substance isolation
> - Expose the injury and assess the bleeding source.
> - Apply direct pressure to the bleeding source, use dressing from the casualty's IFAK or JFAK.
> - Pack wound tightly with the hemostatic gauze until the wound cavity is filled. Note . These steps should be performed within 90 seconds.
> - Ensure that the gauze extends 1 to 2 inches above the skin.
> - Hold pressure for a minimum of three minutes.
> - Reassess to ensure bleeding has been controlled while maintaining pressure.
> - If bleeding has not been controlled.
> - If packed with hemostatic dressing, and extra hemostatic dressings are available, remove and repack.
> - If packed with gauze or initially packed with hemostatic dressing and no more hemostatic dressing is available, apply additional gauze and pressure (for three minutes) until the bleeding stops.
> - Place an elastic bandage over the dressing leaving enough tail to tie it into a knot.
> - While maintaining pressure, wrap diagonally across the chest under the opposite arm (armpit) around the back to the neck and back over the wound.
> - Wrap the bandage (no less than 1 1?2 times) over the packing material covering it completely.
> - Wrap around the neck and under the arm (on alternating sides of the tail, while maintaining tension/pressure), pulling elastic bandage tightly for pressure, covering the packing material. Note. Do not use the pressure bar on the neck.
> - Secure the dressing by tying a non-slip knot with the end of the elastic bandage and its tail.
> - Secure the elastic bandage tails with tape, wrapping a minimum of 1 1?2 times around the knot.
> - Swath the upper arm (of the injured side) to the chest using a bandage.
> - Continue to assess the wound for further bleeding.
> - Document all findings and treatments on DD Form 1380.

## Armpit bleeding  `bleeding/armpit`

Voice: armpit bleeding, bleeding from the armpit, bleeding shoulder

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-77**

> The axillary (upper armpit) area is an area where the arm attaches to the torso. The axillary artery runs from the torso through the armpit and feeds oxygenated blood to both arms. Axillary junctional hemorrhage is very difficult to treat if attention to detail is not observed. The steps for controlling Axillary Junctional Hemorrhage are:
> - Consider body substance isolation.
> - Cut away any clothing (to expose injury) and lift the arm to assess the bleeding source.
> - Apply direct pressure to the most active bleed. Prepare hemostatic dressing.
> - Extend the casualty's arm at a 90 -degree angle by placing it on your shoulder.
> - Pack the wound tightly with the hemostatic gauze until the wound cavity is filled. Note. These steps should be performed within 90 seconds.
> - Ensure that the gauze extends 1 to 2 inches above the skin.
> - Hold pressure for a minimum of three minutes.
> - If the bleeding has not been controlled.
> - If packed with hemostatic dressing, and extra hemostatic dressings are available, remove and repack starting at step 5.
> - If packed with gauze or initially packed with a hemostatic dressing and no more hemostatic dressing is available, apply additional gauze and pressure (for three minutes) until bleeding stops. Note. Clothing may need to be cut away to properly expose the injury. The best position to treat the casualty is the seated position. If the casualty cannot be treated in the seated position, you will need to sit the casualty up as much as possible to apply the elastic bandage.
> - While maintaining pressure on the dressing/gauze, wrap the pressure (or elastic) bandage around the injured shoulder twice to ensure the gauze underneath is completely covered.
> - Wrap the elastic bandage across, back and under the opposite armpit, anchoring around the opposite shoulder in a "figure 8" pattern.
> - Depending on the bandage used, secure with the closure bar of tie tails of elastic bandage with a nonslip knot.
> - Secure the pressure (elastic) bandage tails and knot using tape, wrapping a minimum of 1 1?2 times around the knot.
> - Swat the upper arm to the side of the chest using a cravat.
> - Continue to assess the wound for further bleeding.

## Groin bleeding  `bleeding/groin`

Voice: groin bleeding, bleeding from the groin, inner thigh bleeding

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-84**

> A pressure delivery device (PDD) is made using improvised materials, such as a shoe or boot, full water bottle, or canteen which applies additional pressure to the wound after it has been packed. A PDD may be needed to apply additional and targeted pressure to control inguinal or groin hemorrhage.

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-85**

> For groin injuries packed with hemostatic dressings, use an improvised junctional PDD to secure the dressing. The PDD is placed in the groin while continuously maintaining pressure to the dressing.

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-86**

> The PDD is then secured with a TQ, which is wrapped over the hips with the windlass or ratchet placed directly over the improvised device and tightened to add additional pressure. Two TQs placed together may be needed when improvising a PDD. Note . Junctional hemorrhages involve larger blood vessels requiring more pressure (and targeted pressure) than can be applied with a pressure dressing alone.

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-87**

> Inguinal (Groin) Hemorrhage Control with Improvised Junctional Pressure Device:
> - Consider body substance isolation.
> - Expose the injury and assess the bleeding source.
> - Apply direct pressure to the wound using a hand, fist, or elbow and open the hemostatic gauze package.
> - Tightly pack the wound with hemostatic gauze until the wound cavity is filled (finishing the packing within 90 seconds).
> - Hold pressure for a minimum of 3 minutes.
> - Reassess to ensure that the bleeding has been controlled while maintaining pressure.
> - If packed with hemostatic dressing, and extra hemostatic dressings are available, remove and repack.
> - If packed with gauze or initially packed with hemostatic dressing and no more hemostatic dressing is available, apply additional gauze and pressure (for three minutes) until bleeding stops.
> - Select and position a pressure delivery device, such as a canteen, into the inguinal gutter while maintaining pressure.
> - Select a TQ that can wrap around the casualty's waist/hip area or connect the two tourniquets together.
> - Place the windlass or ratchet TQ directly over the middle of the pressure delivery device; ensure that the routing buckle is located towards the middle of the body.
> - Remove all slack from the band or strap using a pushing motion across the casualty's body before tightening the TQ.
> - Tighten the TQ and ensure that the bleeding is still controlled, and the distal pulse is absent. Note . If the bleeding is not controlled and the distal pulse (a pulse located below the TQ) is present, remove any remaining slack in the strapping (if possible) and twist or ratchet the TQ device until bleeding is controlled and/or a distal pulse is absent.
> - Visually inspect the placement of the equipment, ensuring that the pressure delivery device is in place and the windlass or ratchet is properly positioned over the device.
> - Document all findings and treatments on DD Form 1380.

## Pressure dressing and checks  `bleeding/dressing`

Voice: pressure bandage, pressure dressing, bandage too tight, check the bandage

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-70**

> The pressure dressing is placed directly on top of the hemostatic dressing or packing material. While maintaining direct pressure on the hemostatic dressing or packing material, the CLS will wrap the pressure or elastic bandage tightly focusing pressure directly over the wound. Secure the pressure dressing tail either by hooks, or with a knot, and tape both down to ensure they do not come loose.

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-71**

> Pressure bandages should not have a TQ effect. If there is no pulse below the pressure bandage, it has been applied too tightly and will need to be loosened and retied. Another sign that the pressure bandage has been applied too tightly is the color of the skin. The skin will have a bluish tint and may be cool to the touch. The casualty will complain of numbness in the extremity below the bandage. All bandages should be reassessed by --
> - Checking for circulation BELOW the pressure bandage by feeling for a distal pulse (a pulse below the bandage).
> - Checking the skin BELOW the pressure bandage ensures the skin is not cool to the touch, has a bluish tint, or is numb.
> - Checking every time a casualty is moved.

**ATP 4-02.11, para 4-72**

> The pressure dressing is too tight if the pulse below the pressure dressing is no longer present, or the skin is cool. If circulation is blocked or stopped, loosen, and re-tie the bandage. Dressings and bandages should be reassessed and checked routinely.

**ATP 4-02.11, para 7-8**

> It is more effective to prevent shock through hemorrhage control than to treat it once it has developed. Even in cases where shock is already present, the most critical first action is to control any bleeding. Continuously reassess all bleeding control measures, such as tourniquets and pressure dressings, to ensure they remain effective and are adjusted tightly as soon as possible. This is a crucial step in treating shock.
